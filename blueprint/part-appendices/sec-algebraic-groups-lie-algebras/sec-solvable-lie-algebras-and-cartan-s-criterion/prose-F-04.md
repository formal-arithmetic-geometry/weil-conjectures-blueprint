The decomposition $\mathfrak l=Z(\mathfrak l)+[\mathfrak l,\mathfrak l]$
fails for Lie algebras in general, and the radical measures the failure.
For the algebra $\mathfrak b$ of upper triangular $2\times2$ matrices,
the center consists of the scalars and $[\mathfrak b,\mathfrak b]$ of the
strictly upper triangular matrices, so their sum has dimension two and
misses $\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$. Here $\mathfrak b$ is solvable and is
its own radical, much larger than its center; its standard
representation on $\Omega^2$ is faithful but not semisimple, since the
line $\Omega e_1$ has no stable complement. The solvable ideals defined
next are the obstruction, and
Proposition [sk-p-lie] shows that a faithful
semisimple representation forces the radical to be central, which gives
the decomposition.

The derived series of a Lie algebra is
$D^0\mathfrak l=\mathfrak l$,
$D^{n+1}\mathfrak l=[D^n\mathfrak l,D^n\mathfrak l]$.
The Lie algebra $\mathfrak l$ is solvable if this series reaches zero.
The radical $\operatorname{rad}\mathfrak l$ is the sum of all solvable
ideals of $\mathfrak l$. An extension of solvable Lie algebras is
solvable: if the quotient of $\mathfrak l$ by a solvable ideal is
solvable, the derived series of $\mathfrak l$ enters that ideal after
finitely many steps and then reaches zero. Hence the sum of two solvable ideals is
solvable, and $\operatorname{rad}\mathfrak l$ is the largest solvable
ideal of $\mathfrak l$ (Hum, §3.1). We use three classical theorems
from the same book, whose proofs we do not repeat.
