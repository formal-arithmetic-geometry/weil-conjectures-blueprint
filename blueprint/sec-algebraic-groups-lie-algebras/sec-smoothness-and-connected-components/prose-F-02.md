The passage from Lie algebras back to groups rests on one principle: a
closed subgroup of a connected group $G$ with the same Lie algebra is
all of $G$ (Lemma [sk-p-geometry]). For reduced groups this
is a dimension count, since the dimension of a smooth group equals that
of its tangent space at $1$, and a closed subset of the irreducible
variety $G$ of the same dimension is everything. The difficulty is that
stabilizers and centralizers are defined by equations and need not be
reduced. In characteristic $p$, the subgroup $\mu_p\subset\mathbf G_m$
defined by $x^p=1$ is a single nonreduced point with tangent space
$\operatorname{Lie}(\mathbf G_m)$, and the principle fails. Cartier's
theorem (Proposition [sk-i-cartier]) shows that this cannot
happen in characteristic zero: every algebraic group is smooth. The
proof compares the comultiplication with a nilpotent function at the
identity and divides by an integer, which is where characteristic zero
enters. The facts about connected components and images of
homomorphisms in Lemma [sk-p-geometry] are then standard
consequences of smoothness and of Chevalley's theorem.
