Put $F=kK^p$. For a subset $S\subseteq K$, write $\mathcal M(S)$ for the finitely supported exponent functions $S\to\{0,\ldots,p-1\}$, and call $S$ $p$-independent if $(s^E)_{E\in\mathcal M(S)}$ is $F$-linearly independent. The given map $i\mapsto x_i$ is injective: if $x_i=x_j$ for $i\ne j$, the two distinct exponent functions supported at $i$ and $j$ give a nontrivial relation $x_i-x_j=0$ (also in characteristic two). Thus the family identifies with its image $S_0\subseteq K$, and reindexing its exponent functions shows that $S_0$ is $p$-independent.

Let $\mathcal P$ be the set of $p$-independent subsets $S\subseteq K$ containing $S_0$. For the empty chain take $S_0$ as upper bound. For a nonempty inclusion chain $\mathcal C\subseteq\mathcal P$, put $U=\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal C}S$. In a finite linear relation among monomials of $U$, the union of the supports of the finitely many exponent functions is finite. Choose a chain member containing each support element. By repeated comparison, one member contains all these finitely many elements; for empty support choose any member of the nonempty chain. Restriction of the exponent functions to that member preserves their distinctness and monomials, so its independence makes every coefficient zero. Hence $U\in\mathcal P$ and is an upper bound. Zorn's lemma gives a maximal $T\in\mathcal P$; any $p$-independent superset of $T$ contains $S_0$, so $T$ is maximal among all $p$-independent subsets.

Let $L$ be the $F$-linear span of the monomials of $T$. It contains $1$, $F$ and $T$. Products stay in the span: for each exponent sum $e_t+e'_t$, reduce it modulo $p$, putting the removed factor $t^p\in K^p\subseteq F$ into the coefficient; distribute over finite sums. Thus $L$ is a subring of $K$. If $0\ne a\in L$, then $a^p\in K^p\subseteq F$ is nonzero and $a^{-1}=(a^p)^{-1}a^{p-1}\in L$. Hence $L$ is a subfield. It equals the subfield generated by $F$ and $T$: every such subfield contains the monomials and their finite $F$-linear combinations, and $L$ itself contains the generators.

Suppose $x\in K\setminus L$. It is algebraic over $L$, since $x^p\in F\subseteq L$ and $x$ is a root of $X^p-x^p$. Hence $L(x)$ is finite over $L$, and if $d=[L(x):L]$, then $1,x,\ldots,x^{d-1}$ is an $L$-basis of $L(x)$. Express $x^d$ in this basis to obtain a monic polynomial $m\in L[X]$ of degree $d$ vanishing at $x$. Division by $m$ shows that every polynomial vanishing at $x$ is divisible by $m$: its remainder has degree less than $d$ and its coefficients vanish by independence of the basis powers. Thus $m$ divides $X^p-x^p=(X-x)^p$ in $K[X]$. To see explicitly that $m=(X-x)^d$, substitute $X=Y+x$ in this factorization. The least nonzero degrees of the two factors of $Y^p$ add to $p$, as do their degrees; since each least degree is at most its degree, both factors are monomials. Monicity gives $m(Y+x)=Y^d$. Therefore $1\le d\le p$ and the coefficient of $X^{d-1}$ in $m$ is $-dx\in L$. If $d<p$, the nonzero scalar $d\cdot1\in L$ is invertible, giving $x\in L$, a contradiction. Hence $d=p$, and $1,x,\ldots,x^{p-1}$ are independent over $L$.

For $x\in K\setminus L$, we also have $x\notin T$. The monomials of $T\cup\{x\}$ are indexed by pairs $(E,j)$ with $E\in\mathcal M(T)$ and $0\le j<p$. In a finite relation $\sum_{E,j}a_{E,j}t^Ex^j=0$ with $a_{E,j}\in F$, group by $j$ to get $\sum_{j<p}b_jx^j=0$, where $b_j=\sum_Ea_{E,j}t^E\in L$. Step 4 gives $b_j=0$ for every $j$; independence of the monomials of $T$ then gives $a_{E,j}=0$ for every pair. Thus $T\cup\{x\}$ is $p$-independent, contradicting maximality. Consequently $L=K$.

Take $J=T\setminus S_0$ and $y_j=j$ for $j\in J$. The map $I\sqcup J\to T$ sending $i$ to $x_i$ and $j$ to $j$ is bijective: Step 1 gives injectivity on $I$, and the two images are disjoint and cover $T$. Reindexing finitely supported exponent functions along this bijection identifies the combined family's monomials with those of $T$. These are independent by Step 2 and span $L=K$ by Steps 3 and 5. They therefore form an $F$-basis of $K$, as required.
