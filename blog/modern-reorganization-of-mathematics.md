This is a repost of a blog post originally published on the Xena project, hosted by Kevin Buzzard, available at https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2026/11/11/a-modern-reorganization-of-mathematics/

> We mathematicians need to put far greater effort into communicating mathematical ideas. To accomplish this, we need to pay much more attention to communicating not just our definitions, theorems, and proofs, but also our ways of thinking.
>
> — William P. Thurston, *On Proof and Progress in Mathematics* (1994)

Since the beginning of mathematical study, people have been trying to present mathematics and to communicate mathematical ideas. Mathematicians carefully choose names and symbols that match their intuitions in order to improve understanding. For most of this history, the medium for writing down a piece of mathematics has been linear text: a paper consisting of definitions, theorems, and proofs. In modern mathematics, however, the size and complexity of proofs and of the ideas behind them have grown rapidly. These now demand more and more effort both to write and to read.

Various efforts have been made to reorganize and represent mathematics to enhance our understanding. The Bourbaki [[1](#ref-1)] group maintains a series of textbooks developing mathematics in a coherent way. The [Stacks Project](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu) [[7](#ref-7)] is an online textbook in algebraic geometry, in which theorems and definitions are tagged, linked and organized hierarchically by topic. [Kerodon](https://kerodon.net) [[3](#ref-3)] is a similar website for higher category theory. [Kedlaya’s web-based notes](https://kskedlaya.org/prismatic/) [[2](#ref-2)] offer another example in *p*-adic cohomology.

Two further examples are worth noting. The [nLab](https://ncatlab.org) [[6](#ref-6)] is a collaborative wiki with an emphasis on ideas and abstract viewpoints. It is not organized hierarchically following the logical structure of proofs, but around concepts and their relations. [Mathlib](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4/) [[5](#ref-5)] on the other hand is a uniform formal library organized by mathematical field and containing every detail of proofs.

## Two Layers of Mathematics

The last two expositions mentioned above represent two equally indispensable components of mathematics. On one side are the analogies, the slogans, the examples and even the hand-waving arguments that carry mathematical understanding. On the other side are the rigorous, detailed proofs that refine and realize vague ideas into sound arguments. We often think in the first style and write in the second. Consider an example from probability theory. If *X* and *Y* are independent random variables, then

$$\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y).$$

A completely rigorous proof is easy to write down. Yet what guides us in proving and using this lemma is the picture of Pythagoras’ theorem.

We argue that mathematical understanding needs both: High-level ideas and rigorous proofs. Slogans and analogies can convey why a proof strategy may work, but a strategy remains a strategy as long it has not been executed as a complete proof. A text consisting only of slogans walks on clouds. It may hold one up for a step or two, and then one falls. Conversely, a perfectly detailed proof alone does not provide a picture of the whole. This is precisely why reading a proof generated by AI can be so painful. Every step is correct, yet one has no sense of the underlying motivation.

Explaining the high-level picture is similar to telling a story. However, adding storytelling into professional mathematical texts has been hard for several reasons. Choosing and computing a representative example takes effort. Worse, slogans and analogies are very easy to get wrong, and most people would rather not write at all if what they write is potentially wrong. And many proofs are conceptually clear, with the difficulty entirely technical. If one pushes through those technical difficulties to obtain a rigorous proof, that is what gets published. But nobody publishes the other text, saying “here is the argument I first had in mind, here is where it fails, and here is the repair.” This is also why people benefit much more from talking to mathematicians than from reading papers.

The situation has improved recently. Large language models (LLMs) and agents are now extremely good at figuring out proofs and filling in tedious details. The technology of formalization guarantees the correctness of every detail by having a proof assistant check each step. LLMs are also improving rapidly at auto-formalization, to reduce manual formalization work. We are in a position to see AI reduce, rather than increase as at present, the burden of presenting mathematics, so that the two layers of mathematics can be brought together in one place, connected to each other.

## A Prototype

The high-level ideas and the rigorous proof discussed above are the two ends of mathematical presentation. They can be connected by a series of successively refining layers. We want to present this refinement process directly to the reader, so that one can see the overall route of a proof and go all the way down into the details. For a large proof such as the Weil conjectures, one has a top-level picture consisting precisely of the analogies, dictionaries, and strategies described above. A Weil cohomology theory would immediately prove the conjectures, and ℓ-adic cohomology is one example. One then elaborates the argument, working out how each step is actually carried out to obtain a proof at a finer grain. The key properties of a Weil cohomology follow from the calculation of $f^!$ and the general formalism of the six operations on the (pro-)étale site. Step by step, the slogan-level argument is refined into something checkable down to the last detail. Through this process the vague story becomes a completely rigorous proof, and along the way one meets and resolves technical obstructions.

As a concrete proposal, we have built [a prototype](https://weil-conjectures.merten.dev/) that attempts to organize and present a complete proof of the Weil conjectures in this way. It produces an artifact with two halves. The first is a multi-layered text presented as a website, a proof written at several levels of granularity and obtained by refining the top-level argument downwards, so that a reader can enter at whatever level of detail they want. The second is a formal artifact associated with the text, in which every step at the finest level is checked by a proof assistant, so that the whole proof is machine-verified.

This layered structure also determines the division of labour between humans and AI. Humans discuss and decide the overall proof route ([see this detailed discussion](https://blueprint.fuxi.merten.dev/#/blog/proof-route-choices)), as well as the underlying key definitions and mathematical frameworks. As the argument is refined, LLMs begin to fill in the details of key theorems fixed by hand, while humans review and adjust. At the bottom, AI takes the human-readable but not yet fully rigorous text and expands every last detail on its own, then checks the result by formalization. Mathematical judgement thus sets the route, and AI makes it practical to carry the details all the way down to a machine-checked proof.

We also invested human effort in formalizing the main framework of derived categories and the six operations on the (pro-)étale site, providing an early application in Riou’s work [[4](#ref-4)] on the formalization of derived categories and derived functors, and sharing human experience dealing with potential kernel performance issues ([see this detailed discussion](https://blueprint.fuxi.merten.dev/#/blog/lessons-from-formalization)). The formalization of detailed proofs is left entirely to auto-formalization. The bottom layer is one that a human could, but never has to, look at. People read the layer suited to them, which is compressed, comprehensible, and already detailed enough to show how the strategy actually works, knowing that the layer beneath it has been checked, so the proof is sound.

We use a website to present the notes and the formalization together. It has a graph that displays both refinement relations and proof dependencies. A high-level node can be expanded into a box containing its refining nodes, and proof dependencies are drawn as arrows. Each node corresponds to a page, with high-level nodes carrying the story and low-level nodes carrying precise proofs linked to their formal counterparts. The website thus allows readers to enter at their desired level of detail and move between exposition and machine-checked proof. The webpages can also be reconstructed into conventional linear notes.

All of this has become practical only through recent advances in LLMs and agents. Their mathematical proving ability helps elaborate the layered notes, auto-formalization capability helps complete the formal verification, and coding ability helps build the website presentation. In all three parts, humans only determine the high-level strategy and goals, while AI implements the details. This collaboration allows us to realize a modern reorganization of mathematics.

## An Entangled Web of Mathematics

This is an early attempt to create a library combining both natural and formal language. Although the current prototype is built around a single huge proof of the Weil conjectures, we see it as extensible in the manner of the Stacks Project. Developing a new related body of theory, such as perverse sheaves or nearby and vanishing cycles, means that by the same process of refinement, the new proof decomposes into new parts and reusable parts already in the library. The same low-level details are organized into new high-level stories. In the dependency graph, this corresponds to adding or deleting some blocks while existing low-level blocks move around and regroup into new high-level blocks. Numerous mathematical ideas can be intertwined and linked with the shared details that support them. In this way, we weave an entangled web of mathematics.

We hope that more, and in time perhaps most, of mathematics can be reorganized in a multi-layered way. To make this practical, we need to summarize and share transferable experiences from such projects, so that human effort becomes more efficient and can be concentrated on the highest-level mathematical decisions. Imagine a comprehensive library of natural-language mathematics in which countless top-level core ideas are written down, displayed, expanded, and grounded in detail, and in which the bottom is guaranteed correct by formalization. If we could work on mathematics in this way, thinking and writing at the top level, collaborating with AI to fill in the middle, and letting AI handle the bottom entirely automatically, it might be a fascinating new way to study and understand or even extend the entangled web of mathematics.
